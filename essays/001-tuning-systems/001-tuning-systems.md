# Tuning Systems

## Introduction

The human ear can perceive frequencies between 20 Hertz and 20.000 Hertz. That’s a practically infinite number of pitched sounds that most people can hear.

In music, each note we hear is a pitched sound, but there are relatively few. A grand piano, for instance, has 88 keys, with the lowest vibrating at 27.50 Hertz and the highest at 4186.0 Hertz.

So how do we choose the specific frequencies that will make up the notes in our music? In short: We choose frequencies that sound good when played together.

## Overtones

Most pitched instruments have an interesting property: They produce sound in several frequencies at the same time. We often think of the middle C of a piano as vibrating at 261.63 Hertz, but that's only the note's fundamental frequency.

In addition to the fundamental, that piano string vibrates at several higher frequencies, called overtones. Each overtone is an integer multiple of the fundamental. Overtones have a smaller amplitude (that is, they're quieter) than the fundamental frequency, which is why we can't tell them apart. The shape of the overtones (how loud each one is) gives each instrument it's defining sound, which we often call timbre. If all instruments had the same overtone shapes, they'd all sound the same, so we couldn't distinguish a piano from a violin. If there weren't overtones at all, we'd only hear sine waves.

## Beats

When we play more than one note at the same time, both their fundamental frequencies and their overtones interact to provide a feeling of consonance, that is, how well those notes go together. Many historical and cultural factors influence our sense of consonance, and they change over time and place. However, most people in most places throughout most of history tend to agree on a general rule: the less two notes beat when played together, the more consonant they are.

Say we play two notes on a piano at the same time: the first with a fundamental frequency of 440 Hertz, and the second with a fundamental frequency of 880 Hertz. Here we hear no beating. If we shift the second note's fundamental frequency by 20 Hertz, we clearly hear a beating. Most people would agree that the first pair of notes is consonant, and the second pair isn't.

## Ratios

Using beating as a proxy for consonance, we can arrive at a simple rule: Notes whose fundamental frequencies form simple mathematical ratios tend to sound more consonant than notes whose fundamental frequencies form more complex mathematical ratios. 880 Hertz and 440 Hertz form the second simplest ratio, 2:1. That's the perfect octave. The simplest ratio is 1:1, the perfect unison.

The next two simplest ratios are 3:2, the perfect fifth, and 4:3, the perfect fourth. Next are the so-called imperfect consonances: the major third with a 5:4 ratio, and the minor third, with a 6:5 ratio.
